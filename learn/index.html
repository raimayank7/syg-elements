Learn the periodic table

Short guides in plain language, with real values from the table.

  • How to read the periodic table

    A plain guide to the periodic table: what the tile shows, what periods and groups are, where metals and nonmetals sit, and what the two bottom rows mean.

  • Periodic trends

    How atomic size, ionisation energy, electronegativity and metallic character change across a period and down a group, explained simply with real values.

  • Why is sodium Na? Symbols that do not match the name

    Eleven element symbols, such as Na, K, Fe, Au and Pb, do not match their English names. See the Latin or German name behind each and an easy way to remember them.

  • Electron shells and electron configuration

    What electron shells are, how many electrons each shell holds, how to read a configuration like 1s2 2s2 2p6, and why the outer shell decides behaviour.

  • When was each element discovered?

    A timeline of the discovery of all 118 elements, from the metals known in ancient times to the elements made in particle accelerators after the year 2000.

Where to start

If the periodic table is new to you, begin with the guide on how to read it: what the numbers in each box mean, and what rows and columns tell you. The guide to electron shells then explains why the table has the shape it has, and the guide to trends shows how size, reactivity and other properties change in a regular way across a row and down a column. Those three together cover most of what school courses ask about the table.

For quick revision

The symbols that do not match the English names, such as Na, K, Fe and Au, are a favourite examination question, and they have a guide of their own with the old name behind each. The discovery timeline puts all 118 elements in order of the year they were found, which is a good way to see how chemistry itself grew.

Real values, not just rules

Each guide uses actual figures from the table on this site, so a trend is shown with the numbers that prove it. When you have read a guide, test yourself with the symbol quiz, sort the full list by any property, or open the records page to see which element is the densest, the lightest or the hardest to melt.
