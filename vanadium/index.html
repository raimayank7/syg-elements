Vanadium (V)

Element 23 of the periodic table: a transition metal, solid at room temperature.

← 22 TitaniumPeriodic table24 Chromium →

What is vanadium?

Vanadium is a hard, silver-grey metal. Most of it is collected as a by-product from iron ores and from oil.

What is vanadium used for?

Making steel tougher for tools, springs and axles, catalysts for making sulfuric acid, and large storage batteries.

Where the name comes from

From Vanadis, a name of the Norse goddess Freyja, for the many colours of its compounds.

Place in the periodic table

Vanadium is element number 23. It sits in period 4 (row 4) of the periodic table, in group 5 (column 5), and belongs to the d-block. Just before it comes titanium (22), and just after it comes chromium (24). In the same column, niobium is below it. Elements in one column tend to behave alike.

Family: Transition metals.

Physical properties

At room temperature vanadium is a solid. It melts at 1,910 °C and boils at 3,407 °C, so it stays liquid over a range of 1,497 degrees. Its density is 6 grams per cubic centimetre, so a one-litre block would weigh about 6 kilograms. By density it ranks 58th among the 96 elements with a measured value.

Inside the atom

An atom of vanadium has 23 protons in its nucleus and, when neutral, 23 electrons around it. The electrons are arranged in 4 shells: 2, 8, 11, 2. The outermost shell holds 2 electrons. Written in full, the electron configuration is [Ar]4s2 3d3.

Its electronegativity on the Pauling scale is 1.63. This number shows how strongly an atom pulls shared electrons towards itself: fluorine, the strongest, is 3.98. Removing one electron from an atom takes 6.746 electronvolts (about 651 kilojoules per mole). One mole of vanadium weighs 50.9415 grams, so a single gram contains about 1.18 × 1022 atoms.
