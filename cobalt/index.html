Cobalt (Co)

Element 27 of the periodic table: a transition metal, solid at room temperature.

← 26 IronPeriodic table28 Nickel →

What is cobalt?

Cobalt is a hard, shiny, bluish-grey metal that can be made into a magnet. Its compounds have been used for many centuries to colour glass and pottery deep blue.

What is cobalt used for?

Rechargeable batteries, strong magnets, hard-wearing alloys for jet engines, and blue pigments.

Where the name comes from

From the German word kobold, a goblin. Miners blamed goblins for ores that gave no useful metal.

Place in the periodic table

Cobalt is element number 27. It sits in period 4 (row 4) of the periodic table, in group 9 (column 9), and belongs to the d-block. Just before it comes iron (26), and just after it comes nickel (28). In the same column, rhodium is below it. Elements in one column tend to behave alike.

Family: Transition metals.

Physical properties

At room temperature cobalt is a solid. It melts at 1,495 °C and boils at 2,927 °C, so it stays liquid over a range of 1,432 degrees. Its density is 8.86 grams per cubic centimetre, so a one-litre block would weigh about 8.86 kilograms. By density it ranks 34th among the 96 elements with a measured value.

Inside the atom

An atom of cobalt has 27 protons in its nucleus and, when neutral, 27 electrons around it. The electrons are arranged in 4 shells: 2, 8, 15, 2. The outermost shell holds 2 electrons. Written in full, the electron configuration is [Ar]4s2 3d7.

Its electronegativity on the Pauling scale is 1.88. This number shows how strongly an atom pulls shared electrons towards itself: fluorine, the strongest, is 3.98. Removing one electron from an atom takes 7.881 electronvolts (about 760 kilojoules per mole). One mole of cobalt weighs 58.93319 grams, so a single gram contains about 1.02 × 1022 atoms.
