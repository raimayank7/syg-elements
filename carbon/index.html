Carbon (C)

Element 6 of the periodic table: a nonmetal, solid at room temperature.

← 5 BoronPeriodic table7 Nitrogen →

What is carbon?

Carbon is the base of all known life. Pure carbon takes very different forms: soft black graphite, hard clear diamond, and thin sheets called graphene. Coal, oil, limestone and the carbon dioxide in air all contain it.

What is carbon used for?

Steel making, fuels, plastics, pencil leads, water filters, carbon fibre for light strong parts, and diamond cutting tools.

Where the name comes from

From the Latin word carbo, meaning coal or charcoal.

Place in the periodic table

Carbon is element number 6. It sits in period 2 (row 2) of the periodic table, in group 14 (column 14), and belongs to the p-block. Just before it comes boron (5), and just after it comes nitrogen (7). In the same column, silicon is below it. Elements in one column tend to behave alike.

Family: Nonmetals.

Physical properties

At room temperature carbon is a solid. Reference tables list a melting point of 3,550 °C and a boiling point of 3,825 °C, but at normal pressure carbon does not melt: it turns straight from solid to vapour. Its density is 2.27 grams per cubic centimetre, so a one-litre block would weigh about 2.27 kilograms. By density it ranks 75th among the 96 elements with a measured value. It has the highest melting point of all elements.

Inside the atom

An atom of carbon has 6 protons in its nucleus and, when neutral, 6 electrons around it. The electrons are arranged in 2 shells: 2, 4. The outermost shell holds 4 electrons. Written in full, the electron configuration is [He]2s2 2p2.

Its electronegativity on the Pauling scale is 2.55. This number shows how strongly an atom pulls shared electrons towards itself: fluorine, the strongest, is 3.98. Removing one electron from an atom takes 11.26 electronvolts (about 1,086 kilojoules per mole). One mole of carbon weighs 12.011 grams, so a single gram contains about 5.01 × 1022 atoms.

The ring picture is a simple model that only counts the electrons in each shell. Real atoms do not look like this.
