Manganese (Mn)

Element 25 of the periodic table: a transition metal, solid at room temperature.

← 24 ChromiumPeriodic table26 Iron →

What is manganese?

Manganese is a hard, brittle, grey metal. It is never used alone but is one of the most important additions to steel. The body needs a small amount of it.

What is manganese used for?

Steel making takes most of it. Also ordinary dry-cell and alkaline batteries, and aluminium drink-can alloys.

Where the name comes from

From the Latin word magnes, magnet, through confusion with a magnetic mineral.

Place in the periodic table

Manganese is element number 25. It sits in period 4 (row 4) of the periodic table, in group 7 (column 7), and belongs to the d-block. Just before it comes chromium (24), and just after it comes iron (26). In the same column, technetium is below it. Elements in one column tend to behave alike.

Family: Transition metals.

Physical properties

At room temperature manganese is a solid. It melts at 1,246 °C and boils at 2,061 °C, so it stays liquid over a range of 815 degrees. Its density is 7.3 grams per cubic centimetre, so a one-litre block would weigh about 7.3 kilograms. By density it ranks 44th among the 96 elements with a measured value.

Inside the atom

An atom of manganese has 25 protons in its nucleus and, when neutral, 25 electrons around it. The electrons are arranged in 4 shells: 2, 8, 13, 2. The outermost shell holds 2 electrons. Written in full, the electron configuration is [Ar]4s2 3d5.

Its electronegativity on the Pauling scale is 1.55. This number shows how strongly an atom pulls shared electrons towards itself: fluorine, the strongest, is 3.98. Removing one electron from an atom takes 7.434 electronvolts (about 717 kilojoules per mole). One mole of manganese weighs 54.93804 grams, so a single gram contains about 1.10 × 1022 atoms.
