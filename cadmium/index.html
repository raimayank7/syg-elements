Cadmium (Cd)

Element 48 of the periodic table: a transition metal, solid at room temperature.

← 47 SilverPeriodic table49 Indium →

What is cadmium?

Cadmium is a soft, bluish-white metal. It and its compounds are poisonous, so many of its uses have been cut back.

What is cadmium used for?

Rechargeable nickel-cadmium batteries, some solar cells, and yellow, orange and red pigments.

Where the name comes from

From cadmia, the Latin name for a zinc ore in which it was found.

Place in the periodic table

Cadmium is element number 48. It sits in period 5 (row 5) of the periodic table, in group 12 (column 12), and belongs to the d-block. Just before it comes silver (47), and just after it comes indium (49). In the same column, zinc is above it and mercury is below it. Elements in one column tend to behave alike.

Family: Transition metals.

Physical properties

At room temperature cadmium is a solid. It melts at 321.1 °C and boils at 766.9 °C, so it stays liquid over a range of 446 degrees. Its density is 8.69 grams per cubic centimetre, so a one-litre block would weigh about 8.69 kilograms. By density it ranks 36th among the 96 elements with a measured value.

Inside the atom

An atom of cadmium has 48 protons in its nucleus and, when neutral, 48 electrons around it. The electrons are arranged in 5 shells: 2, 8, 18, 18, 2. The outermost shell holds 2 electrons. Written in full, the electron configuration is [Kr]5s2 4d10.

Its electronegativity on the Pauling scale is 1.69. This number shows how strongly an atom pulls shared electrons towards itself: fluorine, the strongest, is 3.98. Removing one electron from an atom takes 8.994 electronvolts (about 868 kilojoules per mole). One mole of cadmium weighs 112.41 grams, so a single gram contains about 5.36 × 1021 atoms.
